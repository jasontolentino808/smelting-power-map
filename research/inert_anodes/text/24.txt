        The solubility of FeO and FeAl20 4 in cryolite-alumina melts at 1020
      °C was measured. The experiments were performed under an  argon
       atmosphere and in an iron crucible. Both analytical and electrochemical
       experiments were conducted. FeO was found to be the stable solid phase
         at low  alumina concentrations, while FeAl20 4 was found to be stable at
       high concentrations. The alumina activity corresponding to equilibrium
      between the two solid phases was determined as 0.195 or 5.03 wt% ALO3.
      The results are discussed in terms of dissolution mechanisms. The Gibbs
       energy of formation for FeAl20 4 at 1020 °C was determined as - 17.6 kJ
    m o l . The solubility of FeAl20 4 in alumina-saturated melts was measured
        in the temperature range from 980 - 1050 °C. The solubility increased with
       increasing temperature. The apparent partial molar enthalpy of dissolution
        for FeAl20 4 was found to be 64.8 kJ mol'1.



                       INTRODUCTION



  A study of iron oxide solubility in cryolite-alumina melts is interesting for at least two
reasons. Firstly, iron is one of the most predominant impurities present in the commercial
production of aluminium, the Hall-Heroult process. According to Solli et a l (1) dissolved
iron compounds may influence the current efficiency and the metal quality, since they
have  a lower  decomposition  potential  than  alumina.  Secondly,  to  understand  the
corrosion mechanism  of  inert anodes,  for example NiFe20 4, one has  to know  the
solubility of the corrosion products in the electrolyte.


   The main component of the electrolyte used in commercial aluminium electrolysis
cells is cryolite, due to its high alumina solubility. Cryolite has a melting point of 1011
°C according to Solheim et al.  (2). The binary system cryolite-alumina has a eutectic
point located  at 10.0 wt% alumina and 965 °C. In addition to cryolite, conventional
electrolytes contain 5 - 1 3 wt% AIF3, 3 - 7 wt% CaF2, and 1 - 5 wt% ALO3. An industrial
cell with conventional electrolyte usually operates in the temperature range 950 - 980 °C,

  which  is about 5  - 15 °C above the corresponding liquidus temperature for primary
  crystallisation of cryolite-alumina melts.


    There are numerous studies in the literature on the solubility of iron oxides in cryolite.
  However, according to Dewing and Thonstad (3), only a few are interesting since there
  was no control of the oxygen potential in the experiments. Iron has two oxidation states,
  Fe(II) and Fe(III), and even though the material being dissolved is either Fe(II) or Fe(III),
  the solution can contain both. Diep (4) investigated the solubility of Fe(III) in cryolite
  melts under 1 atm of oxygen, using ferric oxide, Fe203, as additive. DeYoung (5) did
  similar experiments using  air. A comparison  of the  results, Diep’s  (4)  data being
  substantially lower than those of DeYoung (5), confirm the importance of a controlled
  oxygen potential. Johansen  (6) investigated the solubility of Fe(II)  in molten cryolite
  under argon and in an iron crucible. We interpolated his data to 1020 °C and obtained a
  solubility of 4.54 wt% Fe(II). Calculations (3) show that at alumina saturation under 1
  atm of oxygen, 6 % of the total iron in solution  is present as Fe(II), while in an iron
  crucible under 1 atm of argon only 0.1 % of the total is present as Fe(III).

  Electrochemical Measurements



     Cryolite-alumina melts (—150 g) were made up by weight from handpicked Greenland
  cryolite and contained in an iron crucible. The experiments were performed in a standard
  closed furnace (4). A thermocouple (Pt-PtlO%Rh) sealed in a platinum tube was used to
  monitor the experimental temperature, which was set at 1020 °C. Argon was purged
  through the closed furnace during the experiment. Ferrous fluoride (Fluka, +95%) was
  added through a quartz tube. The solution was mixed by using a platinum stirrer (15 to 45
  minutes).


   A three-electrode system was employed for the chronopotentiometry measurements. A
  copper wire (0=0.5 mm) served as working  electrode, the  iron crucible  as counter
  electrode and an iron wire as reference electrode. Measurements were carried out by
  depositing iron on a new copper wire after each addition of ferrous fluoride. The active
  area of the working electrode was determined after the experiment by visual inspection
  using a microscope. To avoid the influence of convection, the current applied was of such
  an order that the transition time was less than 100 ms.



  Conventional Solubility Measurements



     Cryolite-alumina melts (—150 g) with excess ferrous oxide were made up by weight
  from handpicked Greenland cryolite and were contained in an iron crucible. Ferrous
  oxide was prepared in the laboratory by heating ferric oxide with excess iron powder at
  1050 - 1100 °C for 48 hours. The experiments were run at 1020 °C, using argon, and the
  temperature was recorded using a thermocouple (Pt-Ptl0%Rh). After the solution had
  melted and the temperature was stabilized at 1020 °C, it was agitated for at least one hour
  using an iron  stirrer. The solution was then allowed to  settle for minimum one hour
  before a sample was taken, using a quartz tube, and subsequently quenched. The samples
  were analysed by ICP (iron content) and LECO (oxygen content). Preparation for the ICP
  analysis was done by  dissolving  1 - 3  grams of a crushed sample  in 50 ml 30%
  AICI3 6H2O. The solutions were kept in teflon vessels at 80 °C for approximately 20 h
  before 5 ml 30% HC1 was added.  Finally,  the solutions were contained  in  100 ml
  polypropylene graduated flasks and filled with deionised water.

  Conventional Solubility Measurements



     In  this part of the work ferrous oxide was used as additive instead of the more
  expensive  ferrous  fluoride,  due  to  the  high  iron  solubility   at  lower  alumina
  concentrations. Ferrous oxide is a nonstoichiometric compound, and known as wustite,
  Fe0.947O. To check the quality of the wustite produced in the laboratory an XRD analysis
  was performed. The  analysis was only  able  to  state  qualitatively  that  the product
  contained wustite. The reason for this was that the sample was not completely crystalline.
  Complete saturation of Fe(II) in the melt was reached within half an hour.

                           DISCUSSION



    The electrochemical measurements, Eq.  [10], give  the stoichiometric  relationship
  between the activity of alumina and the concentration of Fe(II) as 1.22. This  is within
  10% of 4/3, and is in accordance with the reaction mechanism given by Eq. [3] which
  states that ferrous aluminate  is the stable solid compound  at high alumina  activities.
  Additional support is given by the XRD analysis, which showed higher concentrations of
  ferrous aluminate in the lower part of the slowly solidified melt.


    From  the  conventional  solubility  measurements,  Eq.  [11]  and  Eq.  [12],  the
  stoichiometric relations are found to be 0.40 and 1.26. This is  close to 1/3 and 4/3, and
  correspond  to the reaction mechanisms given by Eq.  [2] and Eq.  [3]. The  intercept
  between the two lines is found at an alumina activity of 0.195.


    The results obtained from both the electrochemical and the conventional solubility
  measurements, together with the result of Johansen  (6), are given in Fig. 6. From the
  figure one  sees  that  the  agreement between  his  results  and  the  present work  is
  satisfactory. The results from the electrochemical measurements are somewhat higher
  than the conventional solubility measurements. There are several possible reasons for
  this.  All  calculations concerning  the  electrochemical  part  are performed under  the
  assumption that the ferrous fluoride added  is completely pure.  If this  is not the case,
  which is likely, the value of the experimental points should be lower. Another source of
  error may be the dissolution of the samples when preparing them for ICP analysis. If the
  samples did not dissolve completely, the experimental points from  the conventional
  solubility measurements are too low. Samples from the same experiments were also
  analysed by LECO. Calibration errors connected to the LECO apparatus would influence
  the determination of the oxygen content and thereby the calculated alumina activity.

   A discrepancy is observed between the obtained intercept at alumina activity of 0.195
  and 0.114 calculated from the literature (7,8). This also applies for the Gibbs energy of
  formation. A deviation  is also observed for the slopes giving the stoichiometric  ratio
  between alumina and dissolved Fe(II) and the ones predicted by Eq.  [2] and Eq.  [3].
  There can be several reasons for this.  It was assumed that the activities of aluminium
  trifluoride and sodium fluoride were constant. Another assumption was that the activity
  coefficient of ferrous fluoride does not vary with the alumina content. None of those
  conditions are likely to be fulfilled.


    The effect of temperature on the Fe(II) solubility was investigated. The system under
  study was cryolite saturated with alumina, meaning that the stable iron-containing solid
  product was ferrous aluminate. As shown by Xiao et al (14), the apparent partial molar
  enthalpy of dissolution for ferrous aluminate can be calculated from Eq. [13] by assuming
  Henrian behaviour, and introduction of the Gibbs-Helmholtz equation. The result was
  found to be



                                AH°FeAi2o4 =64.8 (±2.5) kJmol-1                           [16]



    Diep (4) and DeYoung (5) reported the partial molar enthalpy of dissolution for ferric
  oxide to be 124 and 138 kJ m ol1. Since the solubility of ferrous aluminate  is several
  times higher than that of ferric oxide, the obtained value seems reasonable.
